# Argumentation Writing Skills



## Resource
- **Resource - Argumentation**. [Link to Resource](/thinkwrite2/pdfs/Argumentionskills.pdf) 






## 🎯 Learning Outcomes  
By the end of this session, you will be able to:  
1. Tell argumentative writing apart from other essay types (e.g., expository).  
2. Identify the 5 key elements of a strong argumentative essay.  
3. Use block/point-by-point structure to outline your essay.  
4. Write a thesis that addresses both sides of an issue.  
5. Rebuttal opposing arguments with text evidence (no vague opinions!).  


## 1. What Is an Argumentative Essay?  
### Core Definition  
An essay where you **agree/disagree with a debatable issue**, use **reasons + evidence** to support your view, and aim to **convince readers** your opinion is valid.  

### Key Difference: Argumentative vs. Expository  
| Type          | Focus                                  | Example Topic                                  |
|---------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| Argumentative | Take a stand + address counterarguments | “Should stem cell research be prohibited?”      |
| Expository    | Explain facts (no stand)               | “How does stem cell research work?”            |

### Why It Matters  
For exams (TOEFL) and classes (criminal justice, business ethics)—it forces you to think critically, not just share opinions.  


## 2. Unique Trait: Address the “Other Side”  
Argumentative writing requires two extra steps (other essays don’t!):  
1. **Discuss the other side’s reasons** (fairly—don’t twist them).  
2. **Rebuttal** (point out flaws in the other side’s reasons to show they’re weak).  

### Example (From Model Essay: *“Separating the Sexes…”*)  
- **Opposing Argument**: “Single-sex class test scores = mixed class scores.”  
- **Rebuttal**:  
  1. Research is “inconclusive” (some studies show improvement, some don’t).  
  2. Psychologists say confidence (boosted in single-sex classes) matters more than test scores.  
  3. Evidence: Girls in single-sex classes have better attitudes toward math.  


## 3. 5 Key Elements of Argumentation  
Every strong argumentative essay needs these—use the model essay as a template!  

| Element                  | What It Does                                  | Example (From Model Essay)                                                                 |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1. Issue Explanation     | Give background so readers understand the debate. | “Middle school (grades 7-8) is ‘tough’—girls develop faster than boys. Educators debate same-sex classes.” |
| 2. Clear Thesis          | State your stand + (optional) opposing view.   | “Although some oppose same-sex classes, evidence shows they yield positive results.”       |
| 3. Opposing Arguments    | List the other side’s key reasons (fairly).     | Opponents say: 1) No test score boost; 2) Hurts teamwork; 3) Discriminatory.            |
| 4. Rebuttals            | Prove opposing reasons are weak (use evidence). | “Mixed classes are more discriminatory—boys get more teacher attention.”                 |
| 5. Your Own Arguments   | Your reasons + evidence to back your thesis.    | “Single-sex classes = better learning: Girls ask more questions; boys are less disruptive.”|


## 4. Two Organizational Patterns  
Choose based on your topic—stick to one (no mixing!).  

### 4.1 Block Pattern (Used in the Model Essay)  
Group all opposing arguments first, then all your arguments.  

| Section               | Content                                                                 |
|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| I. Introduction       | Issue explanation + Thesis                                              |
| II. Body Block 1      | Opposing Arg 1 + Rebuttal → Opposing Arg 2 + Rebuttal → Opposing Arg 3 + Rebuttal |
| II. Body Block 2      | Your Arg 1 + Evidence → Your Arg 2 + Evidence → Your Arg 3 + Evidence   |
| III. Conclusion       | Summarize your view + final comment (e.g., call for change)             |

### 4.2 Point-by-Point Pattern  
Address one “debate point” at a time (opposing arg → your rebuttal).  

| Section               | Content                                                                 |
|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| I. Introduction       | Issue explanation + Thesis                                              |
| II. Body              | Point 1: Opposing Arg → Rebuttal → Point 2: Opposing Arg → Rebuttal → Point 3: Opposing Arg → Rebuttal |
| III. Conclusion       | Summarize + final comment                                               |

*Best for topics where arguments are closely linked (e.g., stem cell research ethics).*


## 5. Key Writing Techniques  

### 5.1 Write a Strong Introduction  
Two styles (choose one):  
1. **Explanatory**: Start with issue background (model essay style).  
2. **Attention-Getting**: Start with a vivid story/stat (e.g., “In an 8th-grade English class, girls share feelings about *Anne Frank*—boys snicker or snooze”).  

### 5.2 Craft a Thesis Statement  
Use this formula (from the text!):  
`Subordinate Clause (Opposing View) + Main Clause (Your Stand)`  

| Topic         | Example Thesis                                                          |
|---------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Curfew Laws   | “Despite claims curfews control gangs, curfew laws are unconstitutional.” |
| Stem Cell Res | “Although stem cell research needs caution, its benefits outweigh risks.” |

### 5.3 Rebuttal with Evidence  
Use text evidence (no opinions!):  
- **Expert quotes**: “Psychologists say test scores are the wrong measuring sticks.”  
- **Studies**: “Boys get more teacher attention in mixed classes.”  
- **Examples**: “Kids interact with the opposite sex outside school (siblings, parents).”  


## 6. Quick Exercises  
### Exercise 1: Attention-Getting Intro  
**Task**: Write 3 sentences for “Should 17-and-under curfews be enforced?” Use a vivid example.  
*Example*: “On a Friday night, 16-year-old Mia heads to study—but a cop stops her for the 9 P.M. curfew. Her friend walks home alone because her mom fears breaking the rule. This happens weekly in curfew cities.”  

### Exercise 2: Thesis Statement  
**Task**: Write a thesis for “Should companies use local unethical practices (e.g., bribes) abroad?”  
*Requirement*: Subordinate clause (opposing view) + main clause (your stand).  
*Example*: “Although some say companies need unethical practices to compete, refusing them protects long-term reputation.”  

### Exercise 3: Rebuttal Practice  
**Task**: Rebuttal this argument: “English will stay global because most Internet content is in English.”  
*Use text evidence*: 1) Non-English sites are growing; 2) Brazil/Poland pass laws to protect native languages.  
*Example*: “English Internet content will shrink as more non-English sites launch. Plus, Brazil and Poland enforce language laws—limiting English’s spread.”  


## 7. Writing Assignment (600-800 Words)  
Choose one topic—use text evidence for support!  

### Option 1: Manned Mission to Mars  
**Prompt**: “Is a manned Mars flight worth the expense/risk?”  
- **Pro Evidence**: Mars teaches us about Earth’s environment; humans gather more data than robots.  
- **Con Evidence**: Costs $600 billion (800 Hoover Dams); robots analyze rocks safely.  

### Option 2: English as a Global Language  
**Prompt**: “Is English’s future as a global language assured?”  
- **Pro Evidence**: 20-25% of people use English; Internet reinforces it.  
- **Con Evidence**: Brazil bans English in business; Poland bans foreign words without translations.  


## 📌 Key Takeaways  
1. Argumentation = convince, not explain—always address opposing views.  
2. The 5 elements are non-negotiable (issue → thesis → opposing args → rebuttals → your args).  
3. Use text evidence for theses and rebuttals—vague claims fail!
